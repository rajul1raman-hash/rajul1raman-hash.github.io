Case 01No-show analysisReal public data · 110,521 appointments

One in five booked slots goes unused.

01 Presenting problemWhat the operations manager sees

20.2% of booked appointments end in a no-show. The diary looks full; a fifth of it is empty chairs.

Framed as a clinic-operations brief: which no-shows are predictable, which lever is worth pulling, and what we would measure to know it worked. I took a real public dataset and answered it with SQL.

Real public dataset, my analysis. “Medical Appointment No Shows”, Joni Hoppen and Aquarela Analytics, on Kaggle, licence CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. 110,527 appointments at public health facilities in Brazil, 29 Apr to 8 Jun 2016. I loaded it into SQLite, dropped 6 rows (1 impossible age, 5 booked after the visit date) and ship only a 315-cell aggregate here, not the raw file. Build script: tools/build_noshow.py.

02 DiagnosisThree findings, then the dashboard behind them
  1. Finding 1 / lead timeThe longer ahead a slot is booked, the likelier it is wasted. No-show rate climbs from 4.6% for same-day bookings to 33.0% for bookings 31+ days out. Bookings made 8+ days ahead are 36% of volume but 57% of all no-shows.
  2. Finding 2 / SMS remindersThe raw numbers say SMS makes things worse. They are misleading. Patients who got an SMS no-showed more (27.6% vs 16.7%), but SMS was only ever sent for bookings made 3+ days ahead; none of the 50,500 bookings made 0 to 2 days ahead (the safest ones) got one. Compare like with like and the sign flips: SMS recipients no-show 2.8 to 7.3 points less in every lead-time band. Observational, so a hint to test, not proof.
  3. Finding 3 / whoYoung adults miss most, and the weekday does not matter. 13 to 24 year-olds no-show at 25.6%, over 70s at 15.6%. Monday to Friday sit within 19.3 to 21.2%: with this many appointments the gap is real but too small to act on, so I would not build a weekday rule. I also looked at prior no-show history (21.7% for patients with a prior no-show, 21.7% for first-seen patients, 16.0% for patients with prior attended visits only); the data window is only 6 weeks, so history is too thin to rely on and it is not in the dashboard.

03 TreatmentWhat I would recommend, and how to prove it
RecommendationEvidence in the dataHow to test it
1. Reconfirm long-lead bookings. A confirmation message at 48 hours for anything booked 8+ days ahead; unconfirmed slots released to a waitlist at 24 hours.8+ day bookings: 39,774 appointments, 12,750 no-shows (57% of the total from 36% of bookings).Run in one clinic for 4 weeks against a matched clinic. Primary metric: no-show rate. Guardrails: waitlist fill rate, patient complaints.
2. Pilot SMS on the 1 to 2 day band. It is the one band never messaged: 11,938 appointments at 22.7%.Where SMS was sent, recipients no-showed 2.8 to 7.3 points less within each lead band.Randomised 50/50 holdout, because the existing SMS effect is observational. Agree the minimum effect worth the message cost before starting.
3. Offer a flexible slot to the highest-risk group. Booked 15+ days ahead and aged 13 to 39: a reschedule link plus a nearer slot.9,642 appointments (8.7% of volume) at 38.8%, nearly double the 20.2% average. A subgroup found by exploring the data, so validate it on a later period before acting.Track reschedule take-up and the no-show rate of the rescheduled slots.
4. Do not build weekday rules.Monday to Friday range is 19.3 to 21.2%: real but too small to act on.None needed; revisit only if volumes change.
04 OutcomeWhat is measured, and what is a scenario

Measured in the dataset

7×No-show rate of 31+ day bookings (33.0%) versus same-day (4.6%)
57%of all no-shows come from the 36% of bookings made 8+ days ahead
2.8–7.3 ptsLower no-show rate for SMS recipients within every lead-time band (observational)

Scenario, an assumption to be tested

20.2% → 17.9%Overall no-show rate if reconfirmation recovers 20% of the 12,750 long-lead no-shows (about 2,550 slots saved per 110,000 bookings, if the waitlist fills them)

The 20% recovery rate is my assumption, not a finding; recommendation 1 is the experiment that would replace it with a measured number. The data has no cancellation field, so bookings cancelled early are absent and the lead-time curve is partly a selection effect; same-day bookings are low partly because the patient is already there. These data are from 2016 and one health system, so re-check the patterns on the clinic's own bookings before acting.

Case 02E-pharmacy funnelSynthetic numbers · 10,000 visits

Traffic climbing, orders standing still.

01 Presenting problemWhat the dashboard said

An online pharmacy’s visits kept rising while orders did not. Where do people leave, and do first-time buyers come back?

Structured on the kind of funnel and conversion tracking I set up at imcure (Google Ads conversion tracking, partner-API journeys). No real company, customer or order data is shown.

02 DiagnosisEvent funnel and monthly cohorts
Funnel scenario

The biggest leak is not traffic. Browsing drop-off is expected. Among shoppers with intent, 1,180 of 1,900 carts (62%) never reach checkout: the steepest fall after the product page, and the step where shipping cost and prescription upload first appear.

Repeat purchase by first-order month (synthetic)

Retention is improving cohort over cohort (month-1 return 24% → 27% → 31%), so the problem is conversion earlier in the funnel, not loyalty after it.

03 TreatmentRanked by orders recoverable

Three changes, in order: guest checkout (no forced sign-up before payment); prescription upload at the cart, with a clear reason if a file is rejected; refill reminders for repeat buyers. Flip the funnel above to “After treatment” to see the scenario.

Case 03KYC onboardingAnonymised · as-is → to-be

Three hand-offs to say yes.

01 Presenting problemWhat users and operations felt

Customers needed KYC before activation, but the process was manual: documents uploaded, then re-keyed, then checked by hand.

An anonymised illustration of the requirements work I did at imcure. The one measured result is real and on my resume: manual verification time cut by 30%. Every other figure on this case is a labelled assumption.

02 DiagnosisAs-is map: where time and rework sit

Mapping the as-is flow exposes three hand-offs and one rework loop: every invalid document sends the customer back to the start, and an operator re-types data the customer already entered.

03 TreatmentTo-be map, BRD excerpt, user stories

BRD excerpt

BR-3 The system shall extract identity fields from uploaded documents and pre-fill the form.
BR-5 Only mismatches shall be routed to a manual-review queue, each with a reason code.
